# Spanish

Spanish is a pluricentric language with substantial regional variation in pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, and pragmatics. The main focus here is **Rioplatense Spanish**, especially the variety associated with Buenos Aires (porteño), while comparing it with other major varieties.

The Rioplatense area is centered on the Río de la Plata and includes much of Argentina and Uruguay, but **Rioplatense Spanish is not identical across the whole region**. The Buenos Aires variety is one important reference point. A detailed Buenos Aires description in the *Journal of the International Phonetic Association* also notes that related River Plate varieties extend well beyond the city itself. [^1]

## Main varieties

There is no single universally agreed list of "Spanish dialects". A practical comparison for this note is:

| Variety / region                       | Particularly useful distinguishing features                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Rioplatense**                        | voseo, `vos` pronoun, strong yeísmo with rehilamiento in much of Buenos Aires, distinctive intonation, regional vocabulary |
| **Other Argentine varieties**          | voseo is widespread, but pronunciation and vocabulary vary substantially by region                                         |
| **Uruguayan Spanish**                  | closely related to Rioplatense; voseo and yeísmo are widespread, with differences by region and speaker                    |
| **Castilian / central-northern Spain** | `tú` / `vosotros`, distinction between /s/ and /θ/ in much of the area, Spain-specific vocabulary and phonetics            |
| **Canarian Spanish**                   | many features shared with American Spanish, including seseo and `ustedes` for second-person plural                         |
| **Mexican Spanish**                    | `tú` is dominant in most regions; `ustedes` plural; regional vocabulary and pronunciation vary strongly                    |
| **Caribbean Spanish**                  | frequent weakening of syllable-final consonants, especially /s/, with strong regional variation                            |
| **Andean Spanish**                     | different patterns of /s/, vowel/consonant realization, vocabulary, and contact effects depending on country and region    |
| **Chilean Spanish**                    | voseo occurs, but conjugational and pragmatic patterns differ from the Rioplatense system                                  |
| **Central American Spanish**           | several countries have productive voseo alongside tuteo, often with regional differences                                   |

These categories are simplifications. Spanish varieties form continua rather than perfectly separated boxes.

## Rioplatense Spanish

The term **Rioplatense Spanish** refers to Spanish varieties associated with the Río de la Plata region, especially Argentina and Uruguay. Buenos Aires Spanish is the most internationally recognizable example.

A Buenos Aires IPA description identifies voseo, /s/ aspiration before consonants, seseo, and the characteristic rehilada pronunciation of the palatal series among its distinctive features. [^1]

### Voseo

The most visible grammatical feature is **voseo**: the second-person singular pronoun is `vos` rather than `tú` in ordinary informal Argentine usage.

| Spanish variety                 | informal singular                                   |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Argentina / Rioplatense         | **vos**                                             |
| Spain                           | **tú**                                              |
| Mexico                          | **tú**                                              |
| many Caribbean varieties        | **tú**                                              |
| many Central American varieties | **vos** or **tú**, depending on country and context |
| Chile                           | **tú** and regional forms of voseo                  |

In Argentina, `vos` is not merely slang. It is a normal part of the language, including educated and formal written contexts when an informal second-person form is appropriate. The RAE/ASALE describes `vos` as the informal second-person singular form used in Argentina and Paraguay. [^2]

`Usted` is still used for formal or socially distant address. The exact pragmatics depend on context, region, age, and relationship.

### Voseo conjugation

Rioplatense verbal voseo differs mainly in the **present indicative** and **imperative**.

| Infinitive | tú      | vos        |
| ---------- | ------- | ---------- |
| hablar     | hablas  | **hablás** |
| comer      | comes   | **comés**  |
| vivir      | vives   | **vivís**  |
| ser        | eres    | **sos**    |
| estar      | estás   | **estás**  |
| tener      | tienes  | **tenés**  |
| poder      | puedes  | **podés**  |
| venir      | vienes  | **venís**  |
| querer     | quieres | **querés** |
| decir      | dices   | **decís**  |

The RAE gives forms such as `amas / amás` and the corresponding voseo imperative `ama / amá`. [^3]

Important: **not every tense changes simply by replacing `tú` with `vos`**. Many forms are identical or nearly identical, and actual voseo paradigms vary across regions.

### Imperative

Rioplatense affirmative imperatives commonly have the characteristic final stress:

| Meaning   | tú    | vos       |
| --------- | ----- | --------- |
| speak     | habla | **hablá** |
| eat       | come  | **comé**  |
| live      | vive  | **viví**  |
| come      | ven   | **vení**  |
| say       | di    | **decí**  |
| do/make   | haz   | **hacé**  |
| put       | pon   | **poné**  |
| have/take | ten   | **tené**  |

Negative imperatives generally use the subjunctive:

- `no hables`
- `no comas`
- `no vivas`
- `no vengas`

There is regional variation in second-person subjunctive voseo (`vos hablés`, `vos comás`, etc.), so this should not be treated as a single universal Argentine paradigm.

### Pronunciation

Rioplatense Spanish shares many properties with other Spanish varieties, but Buenos Aires has several highly recognizable phonetic features.

### Yeísmo and rehilamiento

**Yeísmo** is the merger of historical `ll` and `y` in pronunciation. It is widespread across the Spanish-speaking world.

In Buenos Aires and much of the Rioplatense area, the merged sound has traditionally had a **rehilado** realization. Modern Buenos Aires speech commonly uses a postalveolar fricative, especially **[ʃ]**, although **[ʒ]** and intermediate/variable realizations also occur. The exact realization varies with speaker, age, style, and social factors. [^1] [^4]

Examples:

| Spelling | Many non-Rioplatense varieties | Buenos Aires / Rioplatense example |
| -------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| `yo`     | [ʝo] or related                | often [ʃo] / [ʒo]                  |
| `lluvia` | [ʝuβja] or related             | often [ʃuβja] / [ʒuβja]            |
| `calle`  | [ˈkaʝe] or related             | often [ˈkaʃe] / [ˈkaʒe]            |

This is one of the strongest auditory cues for Buenos Aires Spanish. Historical research places rehilamiento in the Argentine Río de la Plata area at least as far back as the late 18th century. [^4]

### Seseo

Most Spanish-speaking Latin America, including Argentina, has **seseo**: `c` before `e/i`, `z`, and `s` do not contrast as /s/ vs /θ/.

Thus in Rioplatense Spanish:

- `casa` → /ˈkasa/
- `cena` → /ˈsena/
- `zapato` → /saˈpato/

By contrast, much of central and northern Spain distinguishes `s` /s/ from `z` and soft `c` /θ/.

Seseo is not an "Argentine accent" specifically; it is shared by the great majority of Spanish America. [^5]

### Aspiration of final /s/

In Buenos Aires Spanish, /s/ is commonly **aspirated to [h] before another consonant**:

- `los chicos` → [loh ˈtʃikoh] (schematically)
- `es tarde` → [eh ˈtaɾðe]

The amount of aspiration or deletion varies by speaker and style. It is therefore better described as a **regular tendency** rather than an obligatory rule. The Buenos Aires IPA description explicitly reports regular realization of /s/ as [h] before consonants. [^1]

### Other phonetic features

Rioplatense Spanish otherwise shares many broad Spanish processes:

- /b d g/ commonly have approximant/fricative allophones between vowels.
- /n/ assimilates in place before following consonants.
- /x/ has a regional pronunciation that differs from many European varieties.
- The liquids and rhotics have context-dependent realizations.

Some Buenos Aires descriptions also report a tendency toward **despalatalization of /ɲ/** in certain contexts. This is not something to treat as a universal feature of every Argentine speaker. [^1]

### Intonation

Buenos Aires Spanish has a recognizable intonational profile. One well-known description identifies a characteristic **long fall** in declarative intonation and notes that yes/no questions in Buenos Aires may use a falling boundary where other Spanish varieties commonly use a rising contour. [^1]

Intonation is also strongly affected by sentence type, information structure, region, age, and individual speaker style, so there is no single "Argentine melody".

Italian immigration is often discussed as one historical influence on Buenos Aires intonation, although the exact causal contribution is debated and should not be reduced to "Argentine Spanish sounds Italian". [^1]

### Vocabulary

Argentine and Rioplatense vocabulary differs noticeably from Spain and from many other American varieties.

| Rioplatense / Argentine  | Common elsewhere / English gloss                  |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| `colectivo`              | bus                                               |
| `vereda`                 | sidewalk                                          |
| `pileta`                 | swimming pool                                     |
| `pollera`                | skirt                                             |
| `bombacha`               | women's underwear                                 |
| `valija`                 | suitcase                                          |
| `laburo`                 | work / job                                        |
| `guita`                  | money                                             |
| `pibe`                   | kid / young person                                |
| `che`                    | discourse marker / vocative                       |
| `boludo`                 | context-dependent: fool, idiot, dude, etc.        |
| `bronca`                 | anger / annoyance                                 |
| `pavada`                 | silly thing / nonsense                            |
| `macanudo`               | nice / great / pleasant                           |
| `re-` + adjective/adverb | colloquial intensifier, e.g. `rebueno`, `relejos` |

Not all of these are exclusive to the Río de la Plata, and several have spread well beyond Buenos Aires. The Instituto Cervantes lists many of these as characteristic regional or Argentine usages. [^6]

### Lunfardo

**Lunfardo** is an urban argot historically associated especially with Buenos Aires and the Río de la Plata. It developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in a multilingual urban environment and became strongly associated with tango culture.

Examples commonly encountered include:

- `mina` — woman
- `morfar` — eat
- `fiaca` — laziness / lack of energy
- `cana` — police / police officer, depending on context
- `bacán` — wealthy or socially comfortable person; several senses exist
- `quilombo` — mess / disorder; in other contexts it has more literal or historical meanings

Lunfardo should **not** be treated as a separate language or as the normal name for Argentine Spanish. Many originally lunfardo words entered broader Argentine usage. The Instituto Cervantes explicitly describes lunfardo as an urban argot whose vocabulary has spread into general speech in Argentina and Uruguay. [^6]

### Italian influence

The Río de la Plata region received very large numbers of Italian immigrants, especially during the 19th and 20th centuries. This contributed to lexical borrowing, urban speech, and cultural phenomena such as **cocoliche**, an historical contact variety involving Italian and Spanish.

Italian influence is especially visible in parts of the Argentine lexicon and in the historical development of Buenos Aires speech, but it does **not** mean that Rioplatense Spanish is structurally derived from Italian. [^6] [^7]

Examples of historically associated Italian-derived words include:

- `laburo` — work
- `feta` — slice
- `valija` — suitcase
- `nono` / `nonino` — grandfather

### Argentina is not one dialect

Argentina has major regional differences.

A useful rough division is:

| Area                                                              | Broad tendencies                                                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Buenos Aires / Pampas / much of central and eastern Argentina** | strongly Rioplatense features, including voseo and yeísmo rehilado                           |
| **Northwest**                                                     | stronger Andean influence in some regions; different lexical and phonetic patterns           |
| **Córdoba**                                                       | characteristic local intonation and vocabulary; transitional features                        |
| **Cuyo**                                                          | regional pronunciation and vocabulary; voseo remains common                                  |
| **Northeast**                                                     | contact with Paraguay and Guaraní-speaking regions influences vocabulary and speech patterns |
| **Patagonia**                                                     | broadly related to central Argentine varieties, with local differences                       |

The Buenos Aires description cited by the JIPA article notes that the accent it analyzes is close to the variety spoken by a large portion of Argentina and is also similar to Uruguayan Spanish, but it is still specifically a description of formal Buenos Aires Spanish. [^1]

## Comparison with other Spanish varieties

### Rioplatense vs Spain

| Feature        | Rioplatense                        | Much of central/northern Spain                |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| 2sg informal   | `vos`                              | `tú`                                          |
| 2pl informal   | `ustedes`                          | `vosotros`                                    |
| `z` / soft `c` | /s/ (seseo)                        | often /θ/                                     |
| `ll` / `y`     | yeísmo, often [ʃ]/[ʒ]              | yeísmo is widespread, but realization differs |
| `j` / `g(e,i)` | often a strong fricative; regional | often [x] or related dorsal/velar realization |
| vocabulary     | `colectivo`, `valija`, `pileta`    | `autobús`, `maleta`, `piscina`                |

### Rioplatense vs Mexico

| Feature      | Rioplatense                             | Mexican Spanish (broadly)                                          |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 2sg informal | `vos`                                   | `tú` in most regions                                               |
| 2pl          | `ustedes`                               | `ustedes`                                                          |
| `s`          | can aspirate before consonants          | generally retained more strongly, though regional variation exists |
| `ll` / `y`   | often strongly rehilado in Buenos Aires | usually different palatal realization                              |
| vocabulary   | `colectivo`, `pibe`, `laburo`           | `camión` (bus, regionally), `chamaco`/other regional terms, etc.   |

### Rioplatense vs Caribbean Spanish

Caribbean varieties often show stronger and more widespread weakening of syllable-final consonants, especially /s/, and can have additional reductions of /d/, /r/, or other consonants depending on country and region.

Rioplatense Spanish can also aspirate /s/, but the overall phonetic system is different and should not be collapsed into a generic "Caribbean-style" consonant weakening.

### Rioplatense vs Chilean Spanish

Chile also has a history of **voseo**, but the pronoun and verbal system do not line up perfectly with Argentine/Rioplatense usage. Chilean speech also has distinctive reductions and colloquial vocabulary of its own.

A useful warning is that "voseo" does **not** imply one universal conjugation system across Latin America.

## Common grammar differences

### Second-person system

One of the most useful ways to compare varieties is by person and number:

|              | Argentina   | Spain                 | Mexico      |
| ------------ | ----------- | --------------------- | ----------- |
| 2sg informal | **vos**     | **tú**                | **tú**      |
| 2sg formal   | usted       | usted                 | usted       |
| 2pl familiar | **ustedes** | **vosotros/vosotras** | **ustedes** |
| 2pl formal   | ustedes     | ustedes               | ustedes     |

### `vos` with object pronouns

The presence of `vos` does not mean every pronoun changes:

- `Vos me llamás.`
- `Te llamo mañana.`
- `¿Te acordás?`

The direct and indirect object clitics remain `me`, `te`, `lo/la`, `le`, etc. The major second-person distinction is in the tonic pronoun and relevant verbal forms.

### Lexical preference

Regional Spanish often differs not because one variety has a completely different word, but because each region has a **preferred word**:

| Meaning       | Argentina | Spain   | Other common American usage        |
| ------------- | --------- | ------- | ---------------------------------- |
| bus           | colectivo | autobús | autobús, camión, guagua, bus, etc. |
| suitcase      | valija    | maleta  | maleta, valija, etc.               |
| swimming pool | pileta    | piscina | piscina, alberca, etc.             |
| skirt         | pollera   | falda   | falda, pollera, etc.               |
| sidewalk      | vereda    | acera   | acera, banqueta, vereda, etc.      |

These are preferences rather than absolute rules.

## Pronouns

### Subject pronouns

Spanish normally drops subject pronouns when the verb already identifies the person:

- `Tengo hambre.`
- `¿Venís mañana?`
- `Estamos llegando.`

They are added for emphasis, contrast, clarification, or style:

- `Yo no fui.`
- `Vos sabés.`

### Argentina and `vos`

In ordinary Argentine informal speech:

- `yo`
- `vos`
- `él / ella`
- `nosotros / nosotras`
- `ustedes`
- `ellos / ellas`

`Vosotros` is not part of normal Argentine usage.

## Spelling

Rioplatense Spanish uses the same standard Spanish alphabet and orthographic system as other Spanish varieties. **The dialectal differences are primarily grammatical, lexical, phonetic, and pragmatic, not a separate spelling system.**

However, voseo is represented in normal writing:

- `vos tenés razón`
- `¿vos venís?`
- `vení acá`
- `decime la verdad`

Accents are written according to standard orthographic rules, so forms such as `tenés`, `podés`, `hablás`, and `vení` carry written accents where required.

## Useful Rioplatense examples

### Neutral-ish

- `¿Vos venís mañana?` — Are you coming tomorrow?
- `¿Tenés tiempo?` — Do you have time?
- `¿Qué querés hacer?` — What do you want to do?
- `Vení un segundo.` — Come here for a second.
- `Decime qué pasó.` — Tell me what happened.

### More colloquial

- `Che, ¿qué hacés?` — Hey, what are you doing?
- `No te hagas problema.` — Don't worry about it.
- `Está rebueno.` — It's really good.
- `Vamos en colectivo.` — We're going by bus.

### Very informal / socially marked

- `boludo`, `quilombo`, `laburo`, `guita`, `mina`, etc.

These expressions are highly context-dependent and should not be assumed to be appropriate in formal speech simply because they are common in Argentina.

## Notes on terminology

- **Rioplatense** = relating to the Río de la Plata region.
- **Porteño** = specifically associated with Buenos Aires city and its inhabitants; it is narrower than `rioplatense`.
- **Argentine Spanish** = all Spanish varieties spoken in Argentina, not just Rioplatense Spanish.
- **Voseo** = the use of `vos` and associated second-person forms.
- **Yeísmo** = merger of historical `ll` and `y` in pronunciation.
- **Rehilamiento** = the strong fricative/assibilated realization historically associated with Rioplatense yeísmo.
- **Seseo** = merger of /s/ with the historical /θ/ represented by `z` and soft `c`.
- **Lunfardo** = historical urban argot associated especially with Buenos Aires and the Río de la Plata; many words have entered general Argentine usage.
